What should money buy? Goals before products.

Wing I · Planning · 6 minute read

The film for this chapter is in production

Two senior doctors in the same city, the same specialty, roughly the same net worth. One reviews his portfolio in ninety minutes a year and spends his Sundays with his grandchildren. The other checks prices between patients, holds forty-one different instruments he cannot fully explain, and has not taken a two-week holiday in a decade. The difference between them is not returns. It is that one of them decided, early, what his money was for. The other let his money decide what he was for.

Most investing conversations in India begin with a product. Which fund? Which stock? Is this NCD good? That is like walking into a pharmacy and asking which medicine is best. Best for what? A product can only be judged against a purpose, and most portfolios we see have never been given one. They are not portfolios at all. They are collections: a ULIP bought from a relative, an IPO that felt exciting, three funds from three different bank managers, some land nobody visits.

So before the Library teaches you a single instrument, it asks you the only question that makes the rest of it useful. What should your money buy?

The four honest answers

Strip away the jargon and money can buy exactly four things that matter to a person who has already built a career.

1. Time

The rarest asset for a practising professional. Money buys time when the portfolio is simple enough to run on one review a year, when instruments are consolidated instead of scattered, and when someone else does the watching. A portfolio of forty-one holdings does not signal sophistication. It signals a hundred hours a year of unpaid, unenjoyed work. If time is what your money should buy, the design goal is fewer decisions, not more products.

2. Freedom

Freedom is the ability to say no: to a hospital administrator, to a difficult partner, to another decade of full-time practice. Financially, freedom is built from two materials: liquidity you can reach without penalty, and income that arrives whether or not you show up to work. A crore locked in your own clinic's building is wealth, but it is not freedom. If freedom is the goal, the design favours liquid holdings and cash-flow-producing assets over lock-ins and paper gains.

3. Legacy

Legacy is wealth that survives the transfer. It is partly an investment problem, long-horizon compounding that outlives you, and partly a paperwork problem: nominations that are current, a will that exists, structures the next generation can actually operate. Many large estates in this country lose more to disorder than to markets. If legacy is the goal, the design favours durable, transferable assets and ruthless documentation.

4. Access

The honest fourth answer. Some of what wealth buys is entry: to opportunities that never reach a public platform, to co-investors who can vet a deal in one phone call, to rooms where the real information lives. We include it because pretending it does not exist would be dishonest, and because it is the one outcome money alone cannot buy. Access is purchased with membership of networks, not with a demat account. The Library can describe it. It cannot hand it to you.

The exercise

Take a blank page. Rank the four outcomes, first to last, for the next ten years of your life. Not for an imaginary investor. For you, at your age, with your obligations. A 44-year-old surgeon with school-going children usually ranks them differently from a 58-year-old hospital promoter thinking about her daughter joining the board. Both are right.

Now look at your current holdings and ask of each one: which of my four outcomes does this serve? You will find instruments that serve nothing except the commission of the person who sold them. That discovery, uncomfortable as it is, is the beginning of an actual portfolio.

Why this is the first lesson

Because every later lesson in this Library depends on it. When we teach you to read a bond, the right bond for a freedom-seeker is the wrong bond for a legacy-builder. When we teach you the three-bucket framework, your ranking decides the size of each bucket. Products are answers. This page is the question. Investors who skip the question spend their lives collecting other people's answers.

One warning before you go. There is a fifth thing people quietly ask money to buy: a scoreboard. The pleasure of a higher XIRR than a colleague, the thrill of having caught a stock. The scoreboard is a real human want and a terrible portfolio objective, because the market charges heavily for entertainment. If you want to keep score, keep it on the four outcomes above. They are the only ones that will matter to you in twenty years.

Key takeaways

  • Judge every product against a purpose. A portfolio without a stated purpose is a collection, not a portfolio.
  • Money buys four things that matter: time, freedom, legacy and access. Rank them for your next decade before buying anything.
  • Each ranking implies a different design: time wants simplicity, freedom wants liquidity and income, legacy wants durability and documentation, access wants networks.
  • Audit existing holdings by asking which outcome each one serves. Remove the ones that serve only their seller.
  • Refuse the scoreboard. Comparing returns with colleagues is entertainment, and the market charges for entertainment.
Where DIY Ends

The Library can teach you evaluation. Three things it cannot hand you.

Access to opportunities that never reach the public. The time you spend doing all of this yourself. And someone across the table who is paid to tell you no. That is what the Circle is for.
