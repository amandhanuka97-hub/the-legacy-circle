The three-bucket framework: Core, Yield, Growth.

Wing I · Planning · 7 minute read

The film for this chapter is in production

Ask a wealthy professional what they own and you will usually hear a list. Ask them why they own each thing and the room goes quiet. The three-bucket framework exists to end that silence. It is not a product and not a prediction. It is a discipline: every rupee in your portfolio gets exactly one job, and every instrument must state which job it is doing. Money without a job wanders, and wandering money finds trouble.

The framework divides a portfolio into three buckets. Core, which protects. Yield, which pays. Growth, which compounds. Each bucket holds different instruments, is judged by a different yardstick, and fails in a different way. Mixing up the yardsticks is how most portfolios go wrong: people buy Growth assets and then panic when they behave like Growth assets, or buy Yield products believing they are as safe as Core.

Core: the bucket that protects

Core is the foundation. Its job is stability and liquidity: money that is there, reachable, and boring. Typical residents: liquid and short-duration debt funds, high-quality listed bonds and government securities, index and diversified equity mutual funds held for the long term.

How to judge Core: by cost, credit quality and simplicity, never by excitement. A Core holding should survive three tests. You can explain it in one sentence. You can exit it in days without penalty. And nothing about it depends on a story being true. If a product needs a story, it does not belong in Core.

What Core is not: anything with a lock-in you did not choose deliberately, anything sold to you primarily for its tax wrapper, and anything whose return depends on a single company or promoter doing well.

Yield: the bucket that pays

Yield is the income engine. Its job is cash flow: money that produces money on a schedule. Typical residents: private credit funds, invoice discounting programmes, asset-backed and leasing deals, higher-yielding corporate bonds.

Here is the sentence that protects you in this bucket: a fixed return is not the same as a safe return. Yield instruments feel like fixed deposits because a number is printed on them. They are not fixed deposits. The number is a promise from a borrower, and promises vary in quality. Judging Yield means judging the borrower: what security stands behind the money, where you rank if things go wrong, what the platform or manager has actually recovered in past defaults, and whether the extra return is payment for risk you understand or risk you have not noticed.

Yield rewards diversification more than any other bucket. Ten small exposures across unrelated borrowers is a strategy. One large exposure to a borrower paying 14% is a hope.

Growth: the bucket that compounds

Growth is the long game. Its job is to multiply wealth over years, accepting turbulence as the fare. Typical residents: portfolio management services, alternative investment funds, direct equity strategies, startup and angel investments.

Judging Growth means judging managers and time. The questions that matter: who runs the money and what is their record across a full cycle, not one good year; what do fees and profit shares actually subtract from your return; how long is the lock-in and can your life accommodate it; and how wide is the range of outcomes, because in Growth the average hides the story. Growth money must be money you will not need for seven to ten years. The bucket punishes early withdrawals the way hospitals punish self-diagnosis.

How big should each bucket be?

There is no universal formula, and be suspicious of anyone who hands you one before asking about your life. But the logic runs like this. Liquidity needs in the next three years belong in Core, sized first, before anything else gets a rupee. Yield is sized to the income you actually need the portfolio to produce, not the maximum income it could produce. Growth takes what remains, and only what your temperament can watch fall by a third without selling.

A practising specialist a decade from winding down usually carries a heavier Core than a 42-year-old promoter with a business generating surplus cash. Same framework, different weights. The weights come from the ranking exercise in the previous lesson: time-seekers and freedom-seekers lean Core and Yield, legacy-builders lean Growth.

The concentration rule

One principle deserves its own paragraph, because it is the one this Circle was partly built on. If you work in healthcare, your career, your practice, your equity in a hospital or pharma business, your professional network, all of it is already one enormous, undiversifiable bet on a single sector. The instinct to also fill your Growth bucket with healthcare deals, because you understand them, is natural and dangerous. Understanding a sector and being diversified against it are different things. A sensible discipline is to cap same-sector private exposure at a fixed slice of the total portfolio, in the region of a fifth to a quarter, and to write that cap down before any exciting deal appears. Rules written in calm weather are the only ones that hold in a storm.

The maintenance clause

Buckets drift. A good year in Growth quietly turns a 25% allocation into 34%, and suddenly the portfolio is riskier than the person who owns it. Once a year, on a date you fix in advance, weigh the buckets and move money from the overweight to the underweight. It will always feel wrong, trimming winners feels like betrayal, and that feeling is precisely why the calendar makes the decision instead of you. The full method gets its own chapter in the Maintenance wing.

Key takeaways

  • Every rupee gets one job. Core protects, Yield pays, Growth compounds. An instrument that cannot state its job does not enter.
  • Judge each bucket by its own yardstick: Core by safety, cost and liquidity; Yield by borrower quality and security; Growth by manager, fees and time.
  • A fixed return is not a safe return. Yield instruments are promises from borrowers, not deposits.
  • Size Core first from three years of liquidity needs, Yield from required income, Growth from what remains and what your temperament can hold.
  • Your healthcare career is already a sector bet. Cap same-sector private exposure, around a fifth to a quarter of the portfolio, and write the cap down in advance.
  • Rebalance on a fixed date yearly. Let the calendar, not your feelings, make the call.
