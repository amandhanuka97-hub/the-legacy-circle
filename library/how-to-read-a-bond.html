How to read a bond.

Wing II · Execution · 8 minute read

The film for this chapter is in production

A diagnostics promoter we know was once offered a corporate bond paying 10.5% a year, at a time when his bank's fixed deposit paid 7%. His first question was the wrong one: "Is 10.5% good?" The right question is: "What is the extra 3.5% paying me for?" A bond market is many things, but it is rarely a charity. When someone pays you meaningfully more than the government or a top bank would, they are compensating you for a risk. Your entire job as a bond investor is to find that risk, name it, and decide whether the payment is enough.

A bond is simply a loan you give. A company, or a government, borrows your money, promises interest along the way, and promises the principal back at the end. Everything printed on a bond's term sheet answers one of five questions. Learn the five questions and you can read any bond in the country.

Question one: who owes me this money?

The borrower is the bond. Not the yield, not the app selling it, the borrower. Start with the credit rating: agencies grade borrowers from AAA at the sturdy end down through AA, A, BBB and below. Two things to hold together. First, the scale is steep: the realistic chance of trouble rises sharply as you walk down it, which is exactly why the yield walks up. Second, ratings are opinions, not guarantees, and they are sometimes late. India has watched highly rated names stumble. So use the rating as a starting filter, then look at the borrower the way you would look at a business partner: what do they do, do they earn enough to comfortably cover their interest payments, how much other debt sits on them, and has their rating been stable or drifting downward? A rating that has been downgraded twice in two years is a trend, and trends in credit rarely reverse politely.

Question two: what do I actually earn?

Two numbers get confused constantly. The coupon is the interest rate printed on the bond, paid on its face value. The yield to maturity, YTM, is what you actually earn if you buy at today's price and hold to the end. They differ because bonds trade: buy a bond above its face value and your true return is lower than the coupon; buy below and it is higher. When comparing bonds, or a bond against a fixed deposit, YTM is the only honest number. Also check the payout rhythm, monthly, annual or cumulative at maturity, and remember interest is taxable at your slab, so a 10.5% coupon is materially less in hand for a high-earning professional. Compare post-tax with post-tax or you are comparing costumes.

Question three: when do I get my money back?

Maturity is the promised return date, and the longer it is, the more life can happen before it: interest-rate cycles, business cycles, the borrower's own story. Watch for a call option, the borrower's right to repay early. It sounds generous and is the opposite: borrowers call bonds when rates fall and they can borrow cheaper, which is precisely when you will struggle to reinvest at the old rate. A callable bond's attractive yield may be a yield you never get to keep. Match maturity to your own calendar too. A five-year bond funding a daughter's admission three years away is a mismatch wearing a good yield.

Question four: what stands behind the money?

If the borrower stumbles, this question decides how the story ends for you. Secured bonds have named assets pledged against them, look for the security cover, the ratio of pledged assets to the money borrowed. Unsecured bonds have the borrower's promise. Seniority decides the queue: senior lenders are paid first in a wind-up, subordinated lenders wait, shareholders wait longest. And covenants, the promises in the fine print about how much more the borrower may borrow, are the guardrails that keep your borrower from becoming a different, riskier company after you have lent to it. Nobody enjoys reading an information memorandum. The people who do, own better bonds.

Question five: what can go wrong?

Four ways, and every bond carries some blend. Credit risk: the borrower cannot pay; the big one, covered by question one. Liquidity risk: you need to sell before maturity and find few buyers, corporate bonds in India can be thinly traded, so assume you may hold to the end. Interest-rate risk: rates rise after you buy, and the resale value of your lower-paying bond falls; irrelevant if you hold to maturity, painful if you cannot. Reinvestment risk: the bond ends, or is called, in a world of lower rates. None of these is a reason to avoid bonds. They are the reasons yields exist.

Reading our promoter's offer

Return to the 10.5% bond against the 7% deposit. Run the five questions and the gap explains itself: a mid-sized borrower a few notches below the top rating, an unsecured claim, a three-year term with thin trading in between. The 3.5% is not free money. It is the market's invoice for those exact features. Once he saw it that way, the promoter could make an adult decision: take the priced risk with a small, deliberate slice of his Yield bucket, or decline it. Either answer is respectable. Buying without seeing the invoice is not.

The one-page bond checklist

  • Borrower identified and understood, business, earnings cover, total debt.
  • Rating checked, and its direction over the last two years.
  • YTM used for comparison, not coupon, and compared post-tax.
  • Maturity fits your own cash calendar.
  • Call option: present or absent, and priced into your thinking.
  • Secured or unsecured, and if secured, the cover ratio.
  • Your place in the repayment queue, senior or subordinated.
  • The extra yield over a bank deposit named as payment for specific, listed risks.

Two red flags, in closing, that end the conversation regardless of everything above. Any use of the words assured or guaranteed around a market instrument, and any yield so far above the market that no rating could explain it. High yield is a price tag. When you cannot see what the price is paying for, the answer is not "look harder at the yield." The answer is no.

Key takeaways

  • A bond is a loan; the borrower is the product. Extra yield over a deposit is payment for named risks, never a gift.
  • Ratings are a filter, not a verdict. Check the direction of the rating, not just the letter.
  • Compare on YTM, post-tax. The coupon is a costume.
  • Callable bonds can take back their best years. Match maturity to your own calendar.
  • Security and seniority decide your place in the queue if things go wrong. Read the fine print or accept the back of the line.
  • "Assured returns" and inexplicable yields are exits, not opportunities.
